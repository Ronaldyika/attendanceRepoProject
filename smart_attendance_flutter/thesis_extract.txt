ABSTRACT 
Attendance taking at technology training organisations such as CivilSalt Bamenda is 
mostly done using paper registers passed around the training hall for trainees to sign. This 
method is slow to reconcile, easy to cheat through proxy attendance, and offers  no 
protection against a trainee signing in on behalf of someone who never arrived. Digital 
QR code systems have been proposed as a remedy, but published systems all require a 
live internet connection at the exact moment a trainee scans, and connectivity in Bamenda 
is frequently slow or unavailable during peak training hours, causing existing digital 
systems to fail precisely when they are needed most. This project designed, built, and 
experimentally evaluated a mobile attendance system that performs all fr aud-detection 
and validation logic entirely on the trainee's phone, requiring no server contact at the point 
of scanning. The system uses a Flutter Android application for facilitators and trainees, a 
Django REST Framework backend, a PostgreSQL database, a nd QR codes signed with 
HMAC-SHA256 so they cannot be copied, replayed, or forged. A facilitator opens a 
session and a signed, time -limited QR code is displayed; trainees scan it with their 
registered phones, which independently verify the signature, check  expiry, confirm 
device binding, and check for duplicates before saving the record locally and queuing it 
for background synchronisation. Across a controlled fraud simulation of 120 proxy 
attempts, the system achieved a 98.3 per cent detection rate against  a 95 per cent target, 
synchronised 99.5 per cent of 1,300 offline records against a 98 per cent target, produced 
zero duplicate records, and averaged 1.1 to 1.4 seconds per scan. Compared directly with 
the paper register across five real sessions, it cut attendance time by 71 per cent, detected 
all five staged proxy attempts, and produced zero data-entry errors.

GLOSSARY OF TERMS 
Term Definition 
Device binding 
The practice of permanently associating one Trainee account with exactly 
one physical phone, identified by its device UUID, so that scans from any 
other device are rejected. 
Facilitator The role in this system corresponding to the person who runs a CivilSalt 
Bamenda training session and generates the QR code that trainees scan. 
High-Stakes session A session, such as an end-of-cohort assessment, that a Facilitator explicitly 
flags for the shorter 5-minute QR expiry preset described in Section 3.5.6. 
Idempotency key 
A unique string, combining device UUID, session ID, and scan timestamp, 
used to guarantee that the same attendance record can never be inserted 
twice on the server. 
Offline-first 
A software design philosophy in which the device's local storage is treated 
as the primary data source and the server is used only for synchronisation, 
described in Section 2.3. 
Phone-lending attack 
A proxy attendance strategy in which a registered Trainee hands their own 
unlocked, registered phone to an absent colleague, who then performs a 
scan that passes every device-level check. 
Proxy attendance The act of one person recording attendance on behalf of another person 
who is not actually present. 
Session secret 
A randomly generated 256-bit key, held only by the server and the 
Facilitator's device, used as the HMAC-SHA256 key when signing QR 
codes for a given session. 
Trainee 
The role in this system corresponding to a participant in a CivilSalt 
Bamenda training programme who scans QR codes to record their own 
attendance.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background and Statement of the Problem 
Attendance recording is one of the most basic administrative tasks carried out by any 
organisation that runs structured, cohort -based training. The records that an attendance 
system produces serve several important purposes. Facilitators use them to track which 
trainees are participating regularly in a programme. Programme administrators use them 
to enforce minimum attendance policies that determine whether a trainee can sit for a 
final assessment or receive a completion certificate. At the end of a trainin g cycle, these 
records are consulted when decisions are made about certification, refunds, or continued 
sponsorship for trainees who are on scholarship. They also form part of the evidence used 
whenever a dispute arises between a trainee and the organisati on over whether a session 
was actually attended. For all of these reasons, attendance records must be accurate and 
trustworthy. 
CivilSalt Bamenda is a Bamenda -based technology company that, alongside its 
commercial software, hardware, automation, and renewable -energy activities, runs 
structured cohort-based training programmes in web development, graphics design, and 
related computing skills for young people in the North West Region of Cameroon. Each 
training programme is organised into cohorts, and each cohort follows a timetable of in -
person training sessions held in the organisation's training halls. At the start of every 
session, attendance is currently recorded using a paper register: a sheet of paper or a 
booklet that is passed around the training hall at the start of a session. Each trainee who 
is present writes their name, their trainee identification number, and signs the sh eet. The 
register is then returned to the facilitator and filed. 
This method has several serious problems. The most important is proxy attendance, which 
occurs when one trainee signs the register on behalf of a colleague who is not actually in 
the room. The absent trainee could be anywhere while a friend signs in on the ir behalf. 
This kind of cheating is common in institutions that rely on manual sign -in sheets 
generally. Idris et al. (2017) studied proxy attendance in higher -education and training 
settings and found that it affects between 30 and 40 per cent of all reco rded sessions in 
institutions that rely on manual registers. This means that in a typical training cohort, 
nearly one in three attendance records may be incorrect because of proxy signing, a figure 
--- PAGE 20 ---
 
2 
that directly undermines any certification or sponsorship decision built on top of those 
records. 
Paper registers also have problems beyond deliberate cheating. A register can be lost or 
damaged, destroying weeks of attendance data for an entire cohort. Handwriting is 
sometimes illegible, making it impossible to identify which trainee signed a given ro w. 
Trainees sometimes sign in the wrong row, accidentally altering the record for someone 
else. The data in a paper register cannot easily be searched, sorted, or analysed without 
manually transferring it to a computer, a process that takes a considerable amount of staff 
time at CivilSalt Bamenda and introduces the possibility of transcription errors at the 
point of data entry. 
Digital attendance systems have been developed to address these problems. One category 
of digital system that has attracted a great deal of attention in recent years uses QR codes. 
In a QR code attendance system, the facilitator displays a QR code on a projector screen 
or on their own phone, and trainees scan the code with their own phones to record their 
attendance. This is faster than passing a paper register and, in principle, harder to cheat 
because a trainee cannot sign on behalf of someone who is not physically present in the 
training hall. 
However, the QR code systems that have been published in the research literature all 
share one critical weakness: they require the trainee's phone to communicate with a server 
at the exact moment of scanning. Salleh et al. (2017) and Ibrahim et al. (2019) both 
describe systems of this type, and both require real -time server connectivity for every 
single scan. If the internet connection is unavailable at the moment a trainee tries to scan, 
the scan simply fails and no attendance is recorded. 
At CivilSalt Bamenda, as in many organisations across sub -Saharan Africa, internet 
connectivity is not reliable. Adepoju and Shehu (2014) studied cloud computing and e -
learning in African institutional environments and confirmed that internet outages are 
frequent and often unpredictable. Mtebe et al. (2020) found that network failures directly 
cause data loss and disruption in cloud-dependent information systems used at institutions 
in East Africa, a situation that applies equally to West and Central Africa . At CivilSalt 
Bamenda specifically, the wireless network in the training halls is often unavailable or 
very slow during peak hours, particularly in the early morning and late afternoon when 
many trainees and staff across several concurrent cohorts are com peting for the same 
--- PAGE 21 ---
 
3 
shared connection. This makes any attendance system that requires internet connectivity 
at the point of use fundamentally unreliable in this environment. 
Biometric systems, such as those that use fingerprint readers or iris scanners, are another 
type of digital attendance system. These can sometimes work without the internet because 
the comparison is performed locally on the scanning device. However, they r equire 
dedicated hardware in every training hall, and that hardware needs to be procured, 
maintained, and eventually replaced when it fails. The cost is far beyond what a growing 
technology training organisation such as CivilSalt Bamenda, which operates se veral 
training halls simultaneously and is expanding its cohort intake, can justify for a purely 
administrative function. Unar et al. (2014) reviewed biometric attendance systems and 
confirmed that cost and infrastructure requirements remain the main barri ers to their 
deployment in resource-constrained environments. 
There is therefore a clear and pressing gap in the solutions currently available to 
organisations such as CivilSalt Bamenda. What is needed is an attendance system that: 
(i) does not require the internet at the moment attendance is recorded; (ii) prevents proxy 
attendance without requiring expensive dedicated hardware; and (iii) keeps records safe 
and correct even when a trainee's phone is offline for hours at a time. This project fills 
that gap by designing, building, and evaluating exactly such a system, using CivilSalt 
Bamenda's training programmes as the deployment and evaluation environment. 
1.2 Rationale 
The direct motivation for this project comes from the real problems experienced at 
CivilSalt Bamenda, where the combination of unreliable internet and persistent proxy 
attendance has made it difficult for programme staff to maintain accurate and trustworthy 
attendance records across multiple concurrent cohorts. However, the rationale for the 
project goes beyond solving a local operational problem at one organisation. 
Looking at the research literature, there is a consistent pattern in every published QR 
attendance system reviewed for this project: the system is built, it is shown to work under 
normal laboratory conditions, and the paper reporting it is published withou t any 
measurement of how well the system prevents fraud or how it behaves when the internet 
is unavailable. Salleh et al. (2017) reported good usability scores from students and 
lecturers but provided no data on fraud attempts blocked. Ibrahim et al. (2019 ) reported 
--- PAGE 22 ---
 
4 
high scan accuracy but explicitly acknowledged that their system cannot function offline. 
Neither paper provides the kind of experimental evidence that would allow a reader to 
judge whether the system is actually effective at its primary job, which is keep ing 
attendance records correct. 
This absence of rigorous experimental evaluation is a genuine weakness in the published 
literature. It means that organisations looking to adopt digital attendance systems cannot 
make evidence -based procurement or deployment decisions, because most publish ed 
systems have not been properly tested against explicit, measurable targets. This project 
addresses that weakness directly by defining clear measurable targets before the system 
is built, running controlled experiments at a real deployment site, and reporting the results 
in enough detail that future researchers and practitioners can replicate and build on the 
work. 
The offline-first architectural approach and the HMAC -SHA256 QR-signing technique 
used in this project are also relevant well beyond attendance systems. The same pattern 
of local validation with background synchronisation can be applied to healthcare data 
collection, agricultural extension reporting, field examination management, and any other 
mobile information system that needs to work reliably in areas with poor connectivity. 
This gives the technical contribution of the project a significance that extends beyond the 
specific problem of attendance management at a single training organisation. 
1.3 Research Questions 
1.3.1 Main Research Question 
To what extent can an offline-capable, QR code-based attendance system, incorporating 
device-binding security and a verified synchronisation protocol, outperform existing 
online QR code and manual attendance systems in terms of fraud prevention, 
synchronisation reliability, and data integrity in a low-connectivity training environment 
such as CivilSalt Bamenda?

1.3.2 Specific Research Questions 
1. How can QR code technology be used to create time-limited, fraud-resistant attendance 
codes that a trainee's phone can fully validate without needing a server connection at the 
point of scanning? 
2. How can an offline -first local data storage system be implemented on an Android 
mobile device to ensure that attendance records are reliably captured and preserved even 
when the device has no internet connection? 
3. What device-level and cryptographic security mechanisms can be combined to prevent 
proxy attendance, and what measurable fraud detection rate can such mechanisms achieve 
when tested in controlled experiments at CivilSalt Bamenda? 
4. How can a background synchronisation protocol be designed to guarantee that offline 
attendance records are reliably and accurately uploaded to the central server without 
producing duplicates, even under the unstable network conditions typical of CivilSa lt 
Bamenda's training halls? 
1.4 Objectives 
1.4.1 Overall Objective 
The overall objective of the present project is to design, implement, and experimentally 
evaluate an offline -capable smart attendance system using QR codes and secure 
synchronisation for CivilSalt Bamenda, demonstrating measurable improvements over 
existing solutions in fraud prevention, offline reliability, and data integrity. 
1.4.2 Specific Objectives 
The specific objectives are to: 
1. Design and implement a QR code generation and scanning module that produces time-
limited, HMAC -SHA256 cryptographically signed, session -specific codes that can be 
fully validated on a trainee's mobile device without any server connectivity at the point 
of scanning;

2. Develop an offline-first mobile application capable of capturing, validating, and storing 
attendance records locally using SQLite on the device, without requiring any server 
connection during the attendance-recording process; 
3. Implement and evaluate device -binding and duplication -prevention security 
mechanisms, measuring their effectiveness through controlled proxy -attendance 
simulation experiments covering four distinct attack strategies conducted with CivilSalt 
Bamenda trainees; 
4. Design, implement, and evaluate a secure background synchronisation protocol for 
uploading locally stored records to the server, measuring synchronisation success rate, 
average latency, and data integrity under simulated low-connectivity network conditions 
derived from actual network availability patterns recorded at CivilSalt Bamenda. 
1.5 Significance of the Study 
1.5.1 Practical Significance 
At the most immediate and practical level, this project gives CivilSalt Bamenda, and 
technology training organisations like it, an affordable, ready -to-deploy solution for a 
problem that directly affects programme integrity and certification decisions ever y day. 
The only hardware required is the Android smartphone that most trainees already own. 
No fingerprint scanners, dedicated barcode readers, or always-on internet connections are 
needed. The total additional cost for CivilSalt Bamenda to deploy this sys tem is 
essentially zero, since both the Django backend and the Flutter application are built 
entirely from open-source tools. 
The potential impact on programme integrity is significant. If proxy attendance affects 30 
to 40 per cent of sessions under manual registers, as Idris et al. (2017) found, and if this 
system reduces that figure to below 2 per cent, as the experimental resu lts in Chapter 4 
suggest, the improvement in attendance data quality could affect certification and 
sponsorship decisions for hundreds of trainees across CivilSalt Bamenda's cohorts every 
year.

1.5.2 Technical Significance 
At the technical level, the combination of offline -first architecture, HMAC -SHA256 
signed QR codes, device UUID binding, and idempotent synchronisation within a single 
integrated system is a novel contribution. Each of these individual techniques exists in  
the literature, but no published work reviewed for this project has combined all four 
together and evaluated the combined system against explicit, measurable targets at a real 
deployment site. The specific implementation choices made in this project, including the

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1 Overview of Attendance Management Systems 
The management of attendance in academic and training institutions has changed 
significantly over the past three decades. Researchers have traced this change through 
three distinct generations of systems, each attempting to solve the problems left behind 
by the one before it. 
The first generation relied on paper registers. A register was passed around the training 
hall or classroom at the start of each session for participants to sign. This approach is 
simple, costs almost nothing to set up, and requires no technology at all. H owever, its 
problems are well documented. Idris et al. (2017) studied attendance fraud in Nigerian 
higher education institutions and found that proxy attendance, where one participant signs 
for an absent colleague, affects between 30 and 40 per cent of all  recorded sessions in 
institutions that use paper registers. Beyond fraud, paper registers create practical 
problems: records can be lost or damaged, handwriting is sometimes illegible, data cannot 
easily be searched or summarised, and staff must manually transfer paper records into 
computer systems at the end of each training cycle, introducing the possibility of 
transcription errors at every step. 
The second generation used barcode scanners and local area network (LAN) systems. 
Each participant was given a barcode card, and a scanner at the entrance to the room 
recorded attendance when the card was swiped. These systems improved accuracy and 
reduced some forms of cheating, but they required expensive hardware, a permanent 
power supply, and a reliable local network in every room. In developing countries, these 
requirements made second -generation systems difficult and expensive to maintain, and 
swapping a barcode card with a colleague who then scans it at the door remained a 
straightforward way to commit proxy attendance without any technical skill. 
The third generation moved to cloud-based systems accessible through participants' own 
smartphones. Adepoju and Shehu (2014) reviewed cloud -based e -learning and 
information systems in African institutional environments and found that the shift to the 
cloud brought important benefits: records could be accessed from anywhere, reports could 
be generated automatically, and the data was no longer at risk of being lost in a fire or 
--- PAGE 29 ---
 
11 
flood. However, the move to the cloud also introduced a dependency that proved to be a 
serious problem in African institutional environments specifically: every operation 
required a stable internet connection. If the internet went down, the system stopped 
working entirely. 
Salau et al. (2019) conducted a detailed review of mobile attendance systems deployed in 
Nigerian universities, a connectivity context very similar to that experienced by 
technology training organisations in Bamenda such as CivilSalt Bamenda. They found 
that every single system reviewed assumed continuous and reliable internet connectivity. 
None of them described any mechanism for operating offline or for recovering gracefully 
when the network was unavailable. Salau et al. noted that this was a fundamental design 
limitation that made these systems unreliable in the very environments where they were 
needed most, a finding that motivates the offline-first approach adopted in this project. 
2.2 QR Code Technology in Institutional and Training Settings 
QR codes, which stands for Quick Response codes, are two -dimensional barcodes that 
can encode text, URLs, or other data. They were originally developed in Japan for 
tracking automotive parts but became widely used for general data sharing after 
smartphones with cameras became common. A QR code can store hundreds of characters 
of text and can be scanned from a distance of several metres using an ordinary smartphone 
camera. 
QR code attendance systems work by having a facilitator generate and display a QR code 
at the start of each session. The code encodes something that identifies the specific 
session, such as the session identifier or the training programme code and date. 
Participants open an app on their phones and scan the code. If the scan is accepted, their 
attendance is recorded. 
This approach has several attractive properties for institutional attendance. No special 
scanning hardware is needed, because every participant already has a smartphone with a 
camera. The process is faster than passing a paper register around a large room.  And, in 
principle, a participant cannot sign for an absent colleague, because the absent colleague 
is not physically in the room to scan the code. 
--- PAGE 30 ---
 
12 
Ibrahim et al. (2019) built and tested a QR attendance system in a Malaysian university. 
Their system encoded the course code and the current timestamp into the QR code and 
achieved a scan accuracy of 98.7 per cent under normal lecture room lighting conditions. 
This is an impressive result that shows QR codes are technically reliable for this use case. 
However, Ibrahim et al.'s system required the student's phone to send the scanned data to 
a server for validation every time a student scanned. The server then checked whether the 
code was valid and recorded the attendance in the central database. If the server was 
unavailable or the network was down, the scan simply failed. 
Salleh et al. (2017) developed another QR attendance system and evaluated its usability 
with both lecturers and students. Their results were positive: participants found the system 
easy to use and faster than paper registers. However, like Ibrahim et al.'s  system, Salleh 
et al.'s implementation depended on a live server connection for every scan. The paper 
does not discuss what happens when the network is unavailable. 
Both of these systems also share a security weakness that the present project specifically 
addresses. Because the QR code simply encodes a session identifier without any 
cryptographic protection, a participant who photographs the QR code can share the image 
with an absent colleague through WhatsApp or any other messaging app. The absent 
colleague can then display the image on their own phone screen and have someone scan 
it, or screenshot it and scan it themselves later. This kind of attack is easy to carry out and 
is not blocked by either of the systems reviewed above. 
Zorkadis et al. (2015) studied the security risks in QR code applications more generally 
and identified three main vulnerabilities relevant to attendance systems: QR code 
interception, where a participant captures and shares the code; replay attacks, where  a 
previously captured code is used again after the session has ended; and code forgery, 
where a participant creates their own QR code that mimics the genuine one. The present 
project addresses all three of these vulnerabilities through the combination of HMAC-
SHA256 signing and time-limited expiry, described in detail in Section 3.5. 
2.3 Offline-First Application Architecture 
Offline-first is a philosophy for building software applications where the device's own 
local storage is treated as the primary data source, rather than a remote server. In an 
offline-first application, all the core functions of the app continue to work re gardless of 
--- PAGE 31 ---
 
13 
whether the device has an internet connection. The server is used for synchronisation, 
backup, and sharing data between devices, but it is never required for the basic operation 
of the app. 
Kleppmann and Beresford (2019) provided a thorough theoretical and practical 
foundation for offline-first design. They argued that treating the local device as the source 
of truth, and the server as a secondary store that is updated when convenient, leads to 
applications that are more reliable, more responsive, and better suited to real -world 
connectivity conditions. Their work introduced the concept of Conflict -Free Replicated 
Data Types (CRDTs), which are data structures that allow multiple devices to mod ify 
data independently and then merge their changes automatically without producing 
conflicts. 
The most common technology for local data storage in offline -first mobile applications 
is SQLite. SQLite is a relational database engine that is embedded directly into the 
application. It stores all its data in a single file on the device and does not requ ire a 
separate database server process. This makes it extremely lightweight and reliable. 
Koskinen et al. (2021) tested SQLite performance on mid -range Android smartphones 
and found that it can handle more than 10,000 write operations per second, performan ce 
that is far greater than needed for an attendance system, where a typical training session 
at an organisation such as CivilSalt Bamenda might produce 30 to 60 records. 
The main technical challenge in any offline-first system is deciding what to do when the 
same data has been modified on two different devices while both were offline. For 
general-purpose applications, such as collaborative document editors, this conflict 
resolution problem is very complex. However, for an attendance system, the problem is 
much simpler. Each attendance record represents a single unique event: one specific 
trainee scanning one specific QR code at one specific moment. This event either happened 
or it did not. There is no meaningful way for the same event to be recorded differently on 
two different devices, so the conflict resolution problem reduces to simple duplicate 
detection. The approach used in this project is to assign each record a uniqu e identifier, 
called an idempotency key, that encodes the trainee's device UUID, the session identifier, 
and the scan timestamp. Any record submitted to the server with the same idempotency 
key as an existing record is automatically treated as a duplicate and discarded without an 
--- PAGE 32 ---
 
14 
error. This is simpler than CRDTs and works equally well for this specific use case, as 
Kleppmann and Beresford (2019) themselves acknowledged for event-log style data. 
2.4 Fraud Prevention in Digital Attendance Systems 
Preventing proxy attendance is the central security challenge for any digital attendance 
system. Idris et al. (2017) conducted a comprehensive study of proxy attendance in 
Nigerian higher education institutions. They identified the main methods that participants 
use to cheat digital systems: sharing a screenshot of the QR code through messaging apps, 
using screen mirroring software to project a QR code from one phone onto another, 
physically handing a registered device to an absent colleague, and manually e ntering 
attendance data into a system that does not properly authenticate the submitting device. 
This project refers to this last category, physically lending a registered phone to an absent 
colleague so that colleague can complete a valid scan, as a phone -lending attack, and 
treats it as the hardest of the four strategies to defeat because the phone itself, and not 
merely the QR image, is what is shared. 
Idris et al. (2017) also evaluated several countermeasures. GPS geofencing, which 
requires the participant's phone to be within a certain distance of the training room, is 
effective against remote proxy attempts but can be defeated by sharing a device between 
two people who are both physically near the room. Biometric verification, which checks 
a fingerprint or face scan at the time of recording attendance, is very effective and 
achieves near-zero proxy rates, but requires hardware or platform capability th at is not 
guaranteed to be present or affordable in every deployment. Device fingerprinting, which 
ties an attendance record to a specific device identifier, was identified as a promising 
middle ground: it is cheap, requires no special hardware, and is effective against the most 
common forms of proxy cheating, though, as this project's own fraud simulation later 
confirms in Section 4.1.2, it is not by itself sufficient to close the phone -lending gap 
entirely. 
Arora et al. (2021) developed a mobile authentication system based on device 
fingerprinting that used a combination of hardware identifiers to create a unique signature 
for each device. They tested their system against attempts to spoof the device identity and 
found a false-acceptance rate below 0.3 per cent, meaning that in more than 99.7 per cent 
of cases an attempt to use a different device was correctly identified and blocked. Arora 
--- PAGE 33 ---
 
15 
et al. concluded that device fingerprinting is a practical and effective fraud prevention 
measure that can be implemented using software alone. 
For QR attendance systems specifically, the most practical implementation of device 
binding is to use the device UUID, a unique identifier assigned to each Android device. 
The UUID is read using the operating system's device information API and stored in 
association with the user's account when they first log in. Every subsequent attendance 
record includes the UUID of the device that created it, and any record with a UUID that 
does not match the registered device is rejected. 
Time-limited QR codes add a second layer of protection on top of device binding. Ibrahim 
et al. (2019) showed that making QR codes expire after a short time window effectively 
blocks screenshot-based proxy attacks. Even if a participant shares a screenshot  of the 
QR code through WhatsApp, the code will have expired by the time an absent colleague 
receives it and tries to scan it, especially if the recipient is not physically in or near the 
training hall. The combination of time-limited codes and device binding together blocks 
all the purely software-based attack strategies identified by Idris et al. (2017), although, 
as discussed later in Section 3.5.6 and Section 4.2.1, the length of the expiry window itself 
has a direct and measurable effect on how much ti me a phone -lending attacker has 
available, which is why this project treats the expiry window as a configurable security 
parameter rather than a fixed constant. 
2.5 Cryptographic Security: HMAC-SHA256 
HMAC stands for Hash -based Message Authentication Code. It is a cryptographic 
technique that uses a secret key to produce a fixed-length signature for any piece of data. 
The signature can only be reproduced by someone who knows the secret key. This 
property makes HMAC useful for verifying that a piece of data has not been altered and 
that it was created by someone who possesses the secret key. 
SHA-256 is a member of the Secure Hash Algorithm family. It produces a 256 -bit, 32-
byte output that is computed from any input using a series of mathematical operations. 
The same input always produces the same output. It is computationally infeasible to 
reverse the process and find an input that produces a given output, or to find two different 
inputs that produce the same output. These properties make SHA -256 a reliable 
foundation for security applications. 
--- PAGE 34 ---
 
16 
HMAC-SHA256 combines these two techniques. To compute an HMAC -SHA256 
signature, the sender needs the data to be signed and a secret key. The algorithm applies 
the SHA-256 hash function twice, in a specific way that incorporates the key in both 
rounds, to produce a 64-character hexadecimal signature. If even a single character in the 
data changes, or if the correct secret key is not available, the same signature cannot be 
reproduced. This makes HMAC -SHA256 ideal for signing QR codes in an attendance 
system, because it means a trainee who intercepts or photographs a QR code cannot create 
their own valid code without knowing the session secret. The full mechanics of the 
HMAC-SHA256 computation, and the specific reasons this algorithm was chosen over 
the alternatives considered for this project, are presented in detail in Section 3.5.3 and 
Section 3.5.4. 
HMAC-SHA256 is widely used in real -world security systems. It is the basis for JSON 
Web Tokens (JWT), which are used in this project for user authentication. It is also used 
to verify the integrity of software downloads, to authenticate API requests, and to protect 
messages in many communication protocols. Its security properties are well established 
and it is implemented in every major programming language, including Dart, used for 
Flutter, and Python, used for Django.

2.7 Theoretical Framework 
This project is informed by two complementary theoretical perspectives: the Technology 
Acceptance Model, which explains why users choose to adopt or resist a new information 
system, and the offline-first design philosophy already introduced in Section 2.3,  which 
--- PAGE 36 ---
 
18 
explains how such a system should be architected to remain reliable in the specific 
connectivity conditions found at CivilSalt Bamenda. 
The Technology Acceptance Model, originally proposed by Davis (1989) and widely 
used since in information systems research, holds that a user's intention to adopt a new 
system is shaped primarily by two beliefs: perceived usefulness, meaning the degree to 
which the user believes the system will improve their task performance, and perceived 
ease of use, meaning the degree to which the user believes the system will require little 
effort to operate. Applied to this project, perceived usefulness corresponds to a 
Facilitator's belief that the system genuinely reduces the time and fraud risk associated 
with attendance recording, and a Trainee's belief that scanning a QR code is a faster and 
more dependable way to confirm their presence than signing a paper registe r. Perceived 
ease of use corresponds to how simple the scanning and confirmation flow feels in 
practice, given that Facilitators and Trainees at CivilSalt Bamenda cannot be assumed to 
have any formal technical training beyond ordinary smartphone familiarity. 
The Technology Acceptance Model directly motivated two concrete design decisions 
described in Chapter 3. First, the emphasis in Section 3.4 on keeping the Trainee -facing 
scanning flow to a single camera action followed by an on -screen confirmation, with no 
additional steps such as manual code entry or extra confirmation screens, was intended to 
maximise perceived ease of use. Second, the decision to make the offline validation 
process invisible to the user, so that the scanning experience feels identical wh ether or 
not the phone has a network connection, was intended to maximise perceived usefulness 
by removing the single biggest source of frustration identified in the comparable systems 
reviewed in Section 2.2, namely failed scans caused by unavailable conn ectivity. The 
usability ratings reported in Section 4.1.6 and discussed in Section 4.2.3, of 4.6 out of 5 
for Facilitators and 4.4 out of 5 for Trainees, provide empirical support for the 
effectiveness of these design choices in Technology Acceptance Model  terms, and the 
qualitative feedback summarised in that discussion, particularly the trainee comments 
about the certainty provided by the immediate on-screen confirmation, maps closely onto 
the perceived usefulness construct. 
The offline-first perspective, meanwhile, provides the architectural theory that underpins 
the entire system design in Chapter 3, and is treated here as complementary to, rather than 
in competition with, the Technology Acceptance Model: an application can only be 
--- PAGE 37 ---
 
19 
perceived as useful and easy to use in an environment such as CivilSalt Bamenda if it 
remains functional when the network is unavailable, since a Facilitator or Trainee who 
experiences repeated failed scans during a real session will rapidly form a negativ e 
usefulness belief regardless of how well the interface itself is designed. The two 
theoretical perspectives therefore operate at different levels of the same design problem: 
the offline -first architecture is the technical precondition that makes favourab le 
technology acceptance possible in this specific connectivity context, and the Technology 
Acceptance Model constructs are the lens through which the resulting user experience 
was evaluated in Section 4.1.6. 
2.8 Gaps in Existing Research 
The review of the literature above reveals three clear and consistent gaps that this project 
addresses. 
The first gap is the absence of any published attendance system that combines full offline 
operation, time -limited QR validation, device -binding fraud prevention, and conflict -

systems address some of these requirements but none addresses all four together. Systems

resource constraints of sub -Saharan African institutional environments. Most published

CHAPTER 3: MATERIALS AND METHODS 
3.1 Research Design 
This project used Design Science Research Methodology, commonly referred to as 
DSRM, as defined by Hevner et al. (2004). DSRM is the appropriate research method 
when the goal is to create and evaluate a new technological system. It differs from purely 
empirical research methods, which observe and measure things that already exist, by 
treating the creation of the system itself as a core research contribution. DSRM proceeds 
through six iterative activities: identifying the problem and motivating the research,  
defining what the solution needs to achieve, designing and developing the system, 
demonstrating that the system works, evaluating how well it meets its objectives, and 
communicating the results to others. 
The evaluation phase of this project was planned and designed before any code was 
written. This is an important distinction from the common approach in engineering 
projects of first building the system and then testing it to see what it can do. By defining  
the metrics and minimum performance targets in advance, as shown in Table 3.6, the 
evaluation becomes an objective test of the system rather than a demonstration of its best-
case performance. This approach directly addresses the weakness identified in Sec tion 
2.7 of the literature review, where existing systems were typically evaluated informally 
and without pre-defined performance targets. 
Three separate experiments formed the evaluation phase. The first was a fraud simulation 
experiment in which controlled proxy attendance attempts were made using four different 
attack strategies, each attempted 30 times under supervised conditions. The second was a 
synchronisation reliability experiment in which attendance records were created offline 
under six different simulated connectivity profiles and then synchronised to the server. 
The third was a direct performance comparison with manual paper -based attendance 
conducted during five actual training sessions at CivilSalt Bamenda. All trainee 
participants gave their informed consent before any experiment began, and all results 
were recorded by the researcher without input from participants.

3.5 QR Code Generation and Verification 
The QR code is the central security mechanism of the whole system. Its design must 
satisfy three requirements simultaneously: it must encode enough information for the 
Trainee's phone to identify the session and verify the code without contacting the server; 
it must be impossible for a trainee to create or modify a valid code without knowing the 
session secret; and it must expire automatically to limit the window of vulnerability if a 
code is captured and shared. This section explains, step by step, exactly  how a QR code 
is generated, why HMAC -SHA256 was chosen as the signing mechanism ahead of the 
alternatives that were considered, how the HMAC-SHA256 mechanism itself works, and 
how a code is verified once it reaches a Trainee's device. 
3.5.1 QR Payload Structure 
Every QR code in the system encodes a payload string that has exactly four parts, 
separated by the pipe character. The four parts are the session identifier, the programme 
code, the expiry timestamp, and the HMAC-SHA256 signature. The session identifier is 
the UUID of the attendance session, which uniquely identifies which session this code 
belongs to. The programme code is the code of the training programme associated with 
the session. The expiry timestamp is a Unix timestamp, which is the number of second s 
since 1 January 1970, representing the moment at which the code will stop being valid. 
The signature is a 64 -character hexadecimal string computed by applying HMAC -
SHA256 to the first three parts of the payload using the session secret as the key. 
An example of a complete QR payload string would be: 
3f2a1b4c-8e9d-4a2b-bc5e-
1d2e3f4a5b6c|WDB07|1752480305|a3f9b2c1d4e5f6789012345678901234abcd5678ef9
01234567890abcdef1234 
When this string is encoded into a QR code and displayed on screen, trainees can scan it 
with their phones. The phone decodes the string and then runs the verification process 
described in Section 3.5.5.

3.5.2 Step-by-Step QR Code Generation Process 
The generation of a QR code proceeds through six discrete steps, each of which is 
illustrated in the sequence diagram in Figure 3.5: 
Step 1:  Session initialisation. When a Facilitator creates a session, the backend server 
generates a cryptographically random 256 -bit session secret using Python's secrets 
module, which draws from the operating system's cryptographically secure random 
number generator. This secret is stored in the PostgreSQL database, associated with the 
session, and is never transmitted to any Trainee device. A copy is sent once, over HTTPS, 
to the Facilitator's own device, where it is cached in encrypted secure storage so that th e 
Facilitator's app can regenerate QR codes locally if it loses connectivity. 
Step 2: Payload assembly. The QR/HMAC engine on the Facilitator's device (or on the 
server, for the very first code of a session) assembles the payload base by concatenating 
the session identifier, the programme code, and an expiry timestamp computed as the 
current time plus the configured expiry window, joined with pipe characters. 
Step 3: Nonce and signature computation. Although the payload itself does not need a 
separate nonce field because the expiry timestamp already changes on every refresh, the 
reference implementation additionally derives an internal nonce from a monotonically 
increasing refresh counter to guard against any theoretical replay of an identical 
timestamp within the same second on a very fast refresh cycle. The payload base is then 
signed using HMAC -SHA256 with the session secret as the key, as described 
mechanically in Section 3.5.4, producing a 64-character hexadecimal signature. 
Step 4: Payload finalisation. The signature is appended to the payload base as the fourth 
pipe-separated field, producing the complete payload string shown in Section 3.5.1. 
Step 5: QR image rendering. The complete payload string is encoded into a QR image 
using the qr_flutter package, which implements the standard QR Code (ISO/IEC 18004) 
encoding algorithm, and the resulting image is rendered on the Facilitator's screen. 
Step 6: Automatic refresh. A countdown timer, driven entirely by the device's own clock, 
tracks the time remaining until the expiry timestamp encoded in the current payload. 
When the timer reaches zero, the application repeats Steps 2 to 5 automatically using the 
cached session secret, without any server round-trip. This is what allows the QR code to 
--- PAGE 56 ---
 
38 
keep refreshing throughout a long session even if the Facilitator's phone has no 
connectivity at all. 
3.5.3 Rationale for Choosing HMAC-SHA256 
Several cryptographic mechanisms were considered for signing the QR payload before 
HMAC-SHA256 was selected, and each alternative was rejected for a specific, concrete 
reason relevant to this project's constraints. 
A plain, unkeyed hash, such as SHA-256 applied directly to the payload without a secret 
key, was rejected first. An unkeyed hash only proves that the payload has not been 
accidentally corrupted; it does not prove who created it, because anyone, including a  
Trainee attempting to forge a code, can compute the same hash for any payload they 
choose. This would defeat the entire purpose of signing the code. 
Symmetric encryption of the payload, for example using AES, was also considered. 
Encryption would hide the payload contents from casual inspection, but it does not, by 
itself, provide authentication: without a separate integrity check, encrypted data can 
sometimes still be manipulated in ways that produce another validly -decryptable 
message, and in this application the payload fields are not secret in the first place, since 
the session identifier and programme code are already visible elsewhere in the 
Facilitator's and Trainee's apps. Encryption would therefore add computational cost and 
code complexity without solving the actual problem, which is authenticity, not 
confidentiality. 
Asymmetric digital signatures, such as RSA or ECDSA, were considered more seriously, 
because they would allow the Trainee's device to verify a signature using only a public 
key, without ever holding a secret that could be extracted from the device and used  to 
forge codes for other sessions. This is a genuine advantage over HMAC in threat models 
where the verifying device is untrusted. However, three practical factors weighed against 
this choice for the present system. First, RSA and ECDSA signature verifica tion is 
computationally more expensive than HMAC verification, which matters on the low-cost, 
low-RAM Android devices, such as those with 2 GB of RAM, that make up part of the 
CivilSalt Bamenda trainee population, and where Section 3.10 sets a strict two -second 
scan-to-confirmation target. Second, asymmetric schemes require key-pair management, 
certificate or key distribution, and rotation procedures that add operational complexity 
--- PAGE 57 ---
 
39 
disproportionate to the actual threat this system needs to defend against, which is a 
Trainee sharing or reusing a captured QR image, not a Trainee extracting a signing key 
from another Trainee's phone. Third, because the party that needs to verify a QR co de is 
the Trainee's own phone, and the phone that needs to trust the verification is the same 
phone that later reports the result to the server for independent re -validation, the 
asymmetric threat model of protecting a secret from an untrusted verifier doe s not 
materially apply here: the secret that matters is the session secret held by the server and, 
transiently, by the Facilitator's device, not a secret held by every Trainee device. 
HMAC-SHA256 was ultimately chosen because it directly matches the actual threat 
model. The entity that must be prevented from forging a code is the Trainee, and the entity 
that must be able to verify a code without contacting the server is also, transientl y, a 
device that possesses the session secret only in the sense that the Facilitator's device 
caches it to keep generating fresh codes; ordinary Trainee devices never receive the 
session secret at all, and instead carry the specific signed payloads that we re generated 
using it. HMAC-SHA256 is computationally cheap, verified in well under a millisecond 
even on the lowest-specification test device used in this project, is implemented natively 
and efficiently in both Dart, for Flutter, and Python, for Django, has a long track record 
of production use in systems such as JSON Web Tokens and API request signing, and 
has no known practical cryptographic weaknesses when used with a sufficiently long, 
randomly generated key, as is the case here with a 256-bit secret. It therefore provides the 
authenticity and tamper -evidence the system needs, at a computational and operational 
cost appropriate to the resource constraints of the deployment environment. 
3.5.4 HMAC-SHA256 Mechanism Explained 
Figure 3.7 (referenced conceptually here and shown as part of the QR/HMAC engine step 
in Figure 3.5) illustrates how a signature is derived. HMAC, defined formally in RFC 
2104, combines a cryptographic hash function, in this case SHA -256, with a secret key  
through a specific two -pass construction that is designed to be resistant to length -
extension attacks that would otherwise be possible if a key were simply prepended to the 
message before hashing once. 
The computation proceeds as follows. First, the secret key is padded or, if it is longer than 
the hash function's internal block size of 64 bytes, hashed down and then padded, until it 
--- PAGE 58 ---
 
40 
is exactly 64 bytes long. Second, this padded key is combined, using a bitwise exclusive-
or operation, with a fixed constant block known as the inner pad, and the result is 
concatenated with the message, in this case the QR payload base, and passed through  
SHA-256 to produce an intermediate hash. Third, the padded key is combined, again 
using exclusive-or, with a second fixed constant block known as the outer pad, and the 
result is concatenated with the intermediate hash from the previous step and passed 
through SHA-256 a second time. The output of this second hashing operation is the final 
HMAC-SHA256 signature, expressed in this system as a 64 -character lowercase 
hexadecimal string. 
The security of this construction rests on two properties of SHA-256 discussed in Section 
2.5: pre-image resistance, meaning it is computationally infeasible to find an input that 
produces a given output, and collision resistance, meaning it is computationally infeasible 
to find two different inputs that produce the same output. Because the key is mixed into 
the computation twice, in both the inner and outer hashing rounds, an attacker who does 
not know the key cannot compute a valid signature for a payload of their choosing, even 
if they can observe many valid signature and payload pairs produced by the legitimate 
system, which is exactly the situation a trainee is in after photographing several QR codes 
over a training cycle. 
On the Trainee's device, verification does not attempt to reverse the signature. Instead, 
the device independently recomputes what the signature should be, using the payload it 
just scanned and the session information it has cached, and compares the recomp uted 
signature to the one printed inside the scanned payload using a constant -time string 
comparison, which avoids leaking timing information that could theoretically assist an 
attacker in guessing the correct signature one character at a time. If the two signatures 
match exactly, the payload is accepted as authentic and unmodified. If they differ in even 
a single character, the payload is rejected outright. 
3.5.5 QR Verification Process 
The verification process runs entirely on the Trainee's phone and consists of five 
sequential checks, shown as steps 4 to 6 in Figure 3.5. If any check fails, the process stops 
immediately and the Trainee is shown an error message. No partial records are s aved to 
the database. 
--- PAGE 59 ---
 
41 
The first check parses the QR payload string. The app splits the string at every pipe 
character and checks that exactly four parts are produced. If the number of parts is wrong, 
the code has an invalid format and is rejected immediately. This check catches  cases 
where a trainee tries to type a fake QR code manually or where a QR code from a different 
system is accidentally scanned. 
The second check verifies the HMAC-SHA256 signature using the mechanism described 
in Section 3.5.4. The app reconstructs the payload base, which is the first three parts of 
the payload joined with pipe characters, and computes the expected HMAC -SHA256 
signature using the session information cached on the phone. It then compares this 
expected signature to the one encoded in the QR code. If they do not match, the code has 
been altered, forged, or belongs to a different session, and the scan is rejected. This check 
prevents all forms of QR forgery and ensures that only codes generated by the legitimate 
system for this specific session are accepted. 
The third check verifies the expiry timestamp. The app reads the expiry timestamp from 
the QR code and compares it to the current time on the phone. The code is considered 
expired if the current time is more than 300 seconds, which is five minutes, past the expiry 
timestamp. This five -minute tolerance, called the clock -skew tolerance, is added to 
account for small differences between the time on the Facilitator's phone, which generated 
the code, and the time on the Trainee's phone. Without this tolerance, t rainees whose 
phones have clocks slightly behind the Facilitator's phone might be incorrectly rejected. 
If the code is genuinely expired, the scan is rejected with a message telling the trainee to 
ask the Facilitator to refresh the QR code. 
The fourth check verifies the device UUID, as described in Section 3.6.1. The app reads 
the unique identifier of the current phone and compares it to the UUID that was stored 
when this trainee account was first set up on this device. If the UUIDs do not ma tch, the 
phone being used to scan is not the phone registered to this trainee's account. The scan is 
rejected and a log entry is created. 
The fifth check is a duplicate detection query against the local SQLite database, described 
further in Section 3.6.3. The app searches for any existing record with the same 
combination of trainee ID, session ID, and device UUID. If a record already exists,  the 
trainee has already scanned this session on this device. The scan is rejected as a duplicate 
with a message confirming that attendance is already recorded. 
--- PAGE 60 ---
 
42 
If all five checks pass, the app creates a new attendance record in the SQLite database 
with the pending_sync flag set to true. The idempotency key for the record is computed 
by joining the device UUID, the session ID, and the current timestamp in millisec onds 
with pipe characters. A confirmation message is shown to the trainee. 
3.5.6 Configurable QR Expiry Window for High-Stakes Sessions 
The original design used a single, fixed 15 -minute expiry window for every session. 
During the fraud simulation experiment reported in Section 4.1.2, however, it became 
clear that the risk profile of a session is not uniform: an ordinary daily training ses sion 
and an end -of-cohort assessment session carry very different consequences if a proxy 
attendance attempt succeeds. Following the supervisory recommendation issued after the 
pre-defence review, and consistent with the finding, detailed in Section 4.1.2 and Table 
4.2, that every successful phone-lending attack observed in the fraud simulation occurred 
within the first two minutes of the fifteen-minute window, the expiry window was made 
a configurable, per-session parameter rather than a fixed constant. 
Concretely, FR-16 in Table 3.1 is implemented by adding an expiry_window_seconds 
field to the Session model, shown in the class diagram in Figure 3.3, which the Facilitator 
sets when creating a session. Two presets are offered in the session creation scree n: a 
Standard preset of 900 seconds, 15 minutes, retained as the default for ordinary daily 
training sessions where the operational convenience of a longer window outweighs the 
marginal fraud risk; and a High -Stakes preset of 300 seconds, 5 minutes, intend ed for 
sessions that a Facilitator explicitly flags as examination -linked or otherwise 
consequential, such as a final cohort assessment or a sponsor -visible attendance record. 
A Facilitator may also enter a custom value between 60 and 900 seconds if neither preset 
fits a particular session. Reducing the window does not change any of the five verification 
checks in Section 3.5.5; it only changes the value compared against in the third check, so 
the security benefit is obtained without any additional implemen tation complexity or 
performance cost.

3.6 Security Mechanisms 
Beyond the HMAC-SHA256 signing scheme described in Section 3.5, the system relies 
on four further security mechanisms working together: device binding, JWT -based 
authentication, local duplicate prevention, and independent server -side re -validation. 
Each mechanism defends against a different point at which the system could otherwise 
be attacked, and together they are what allow the system to reach the fraud -detection 
results reported in Section 4.1.2. 
3.6.1 Device Binding 
Device binding ties a Trainee's account to exactly one physical Android device. When a 
Trainee logs into the mobile application for the first time, the app reads a device identifier 
using the device_info_plus package, which returns a value derived from the  Android ID 
assigned by the operating system. This identifier is sent to the server and stored as the 
registered_device_uuid field on the trainee's account, visible on the Trainee class in the 
class diagram in Figure 3.3. Every subsequent attendance record generated on that device 
includes the same identifier, and the fourth verification check described in Section 3.5.5 
rejects any scan performed from a device whose identifier does not match the one on file. 
If a trainee genuinely changes phones, an Administrator can rebind the account to the new 
device through FR-13 in Table 3.1. Every rebinding action is written to an audit log that 
records the old device identifier, the new device identifier, the Administra tor who 
performed the change, and a timestamp, which allows any pattern of suspiciously frequent 
rebinding, potentially indicating an attempt to game the system, to be identified during 
routine review. 
3.6.2 JWT Authentication 
Access to the backend API requires a valid JSON Web Token, obtained by submitting a 
username and password to the authentication endpoint. A JWT is itself an HMAC -
SHA256-signed structure, in this implementation, meaning the same underlying 
cryptographic technique used for QR codes also protects the API session layer. The token 
encodes the user's identifier, role, and an expiry time, and is sent in the Authorization 
header of every subsequent API request. Tokens are stored only in the device's encrypted 
--- PAGE 62 ---
 
44 
secure storage, satisfying NFR-8 in Table 3.2, and are never written to plain application 
logs. 
3.6.3 Local Duplicate Prevention 
The fifth check in Section 3.5.5 queries the local SQLite database for an existing record 
matching the same trainee, session, and device before a new record is created. This is 
enforced both at the application logic level and, redundantly, by a unique cons traint on 
the local database table itself, so that even a bug in the application logic could not 
accidentally create a duplicate row locally. This redundancy at the storage layer mirrors 
the redundancy later applied server-side, described in Section 3.6.4, and reflects a general 
design principle followed throughout the system: correctness -critical checks are never 
relied upon in only one place. 
3.6.4 Server-Side Re-Validation 
Every record that reaches the server through the synchronisation protocol in Section 3.8 
is re-validated independently, exactly as shown in the Phase 2 half of Figure 3.5 and in 
the server re -validation pipeline block of the architecture diagram in Figure 3.1. The 
server does not simply trust that a record arriving from a device is valid because the device 
claims to have already checked it. Instead, the server repeats the signature verification 
using its own copy of the session secret, checks that the devic e UUID in the record 
matches the device UUID on file for that trainee, checks that the scan timestamp falls 
within a permitted window of the session's lifetime, and finally attempts to insert the 
record subject to a PostgreSQL UNIQUE constraint on the comb ination of trainee, 
session, and device. 
This server-side re-validation exists for two reasons. The first is defence in depth: if a 
modified or tampered version of the mobile application were somehow installed on a 
device and made to skip its own local checks, the server would still catch an inva lid 
record. The second reason is that the server is the only party in the system with a complete, 
trusted view of session state; a device that has been offline for a long period may hold 
stale cached information, and the server-side check is what ultimately determines whether 
a record becomes the authoritative version stored in PostgreSQL. Any record that fails 
server-side re-validation is written to the integrity_log table defined in FR -14, together 
--- PAGE 63 ---
 
45 
with the specific reason for rejection, and any case where two different devices both 
attempt to submit a record for the same trainee and session, which is not possible under 
normal single -device operation but can be triggered by a genuine device rebinding  
overlapping with an unsynced record from the old device, is written to the conflict_log 
and flagged for Facilitator review under FR-15. 
3.6.5 Feasibility of a Future Biometric Confirmation Step 
The fraud simulation results discussed in Section 4.1.2 and Section 4.2.1 show that the 
combination of HMAC-SHA256 signing, expiry, and device binding blocks every purely 
software-based attack strategy but does not, on its own, fully close what this project calls 
the phone -lending gap: a scenario in which a Trainee physically hands their own 
registered, already -unlocked phone to an absent colleague, who then performs a 
completely legitimate-looking scan from the correct, registered device. Because every 
check in Section 3.5.5 passes in this scenario, the current system cannot distinguish a 
phone-lending attack from a genuine scan using only the mechanisms described so far. 
This is not a flaw specific to the implementation; it follows directly from the fact t hat 
device binding authenticates a device, not the person holding it, and no software -only 
mechanism running on a general -purpose Android phone can, by itself, prove who is 
physically holding it at the moment of a scan. 
Following the supervisory recommendation issued after the pre -defence review, this 
subsection sets out, without implementing it in the present system, a concrete and feasible 
path to closing this gap through a biometric confirmation step at scan time. The proposed 
mechanism uses Flutter's local_auth package, which provides a unified interface to the 
fingerprint and face-recognition hardware already built into the overwhelming majority 
of Android phones sold in Cameroon in the last five years, including the low-cost devices 
typical of the CivilSalt Bamenda trainee population. The local_auth package does not 
transmit any biometric data off the device and does not require the application to store or 
process raw biometric templates at all; it simply asks the ope rating system's existing 
biometric subsystem, the same one used to unlock the phone, to confirm that the person 
currently holding the device is the person enrolled as its owner, and returns a boolean 
result to the application. 
--- PAGE 64 ---
 
46 
Integrating this into the verification pipeline in Section 3.5.5 would require inserting a 
sixth check, immediately after the device UUID check, that calls local_auth and requires 
a successful biometric confirmation before the scan can proceed to the duplicate-detection 
check and record creation. Because the confirmation happens through the operating 
system's own secure biometric hardware rather than through any comparison performed 
by this application's own code, no new cryptographic material or server -side 
infrastructure would be required, and the addition would not increase server load or 
require any change to the synchronisation protocol in Section 3.8. 
Three practical considerations make this a feasible near -term extension rather than a 
purely theoretical one. First, device coverage: informal inspection of the trainee device 
models observed during the evaluation described in Section 3.10 found that the g reat 
majority already have a functioning fingerprint sensor, and local_auth degrades 
gracefully on devices that lack one, allowing a policy decision about whether to make the 
step mandatory or optional per device. Second, added interaction time: a fingerpr int 
confirmation on modern Android hardware typically completes in under one second, 
which would not be expected to breach the two-second scan-to-confirmation target set in 
NFR-1, though this would need to be confirmed experimentally rather than assumed. 
Third, this mechanism specifically and completely closes the phone-lending gap, because 
it requires the biometric owner of the specific device to be physically present and 
cooperating at the moment of the scan, which is precisely the property that device binding 
alone cannot provide. For these reasons, adding a Flutter local_auth confirmation step is 
recommended as the immediate next development priority for any deployment of this 
system to sessions where the residual phone -lending risk is judged unacceptabl e, and is 
carried forward as a specific, concrete recommendation in Section 5.2.

3.8 Synchronisation Protocol 
The synchronisation protocol is responsible for moving attendance records that were 
created while a device was offline into the authoritative PostgreSQL database once 
connectivity returns, without ever creating duplicates and without losing any record even 
if a synchronisation attempt is interrupted partway through. Figure 3.10, referenced here 
and shown in narrative form as Phase 2 of Figure 3.5 and the lower flow of the sync 
diagram, describes the protocol as a finite state machine with four states for each record: 
Pending, Uploading, Synced, and Rejected. 
A record starts in the Pending state as soon as it is created locally, as described in Section 
3.5.5. A background service, implemented using Flutter's connectivity_plus package, 
continuously monitors the device's network status. As soon as a connection be comes 
available, the service collects every record still in the Pending state and moves them to 
the Uploading state, then sends them as a single batch in one HTTPS POST request to the 
/api/v1/sync/batch/ endpoint, rather than sending one request per record , which reduces 
the total number of round trips required and is particularly important given the unreliable 
network conditions typical of CivilSalt Bamenda. 
On the server, each record in the batch passes through the re-validation pipeline described 
in Section 3.6.4. Records that pass validation and are not duplicates are inserted into the 
attendance_records table and marked as authoritative. The server's respo nse lists which 
records were accepted, which were rejected and why, and which were recognised as 
duplicates of records already stored. The device uses this response to move each record 
into either the Synced or Rejected state. Records that are recognised as duplicates are also 
moved to the Synced state, on the reasoning that the underlying attendance event has 
already been correctly recorded, even though this particular submission was redundant. 
If the network connection is lost partway through the upload, before a response is 
received, the affected records remain in the Uploading state and are automatically retried 
the next time connectivity is confirmed. Because every record carries the idempote ncy 
key described in Section 3.5.5, which combines the device UUID, session ID, and 
creation timestamp, and because the server enforces a UNIQUE constraint on this key in 
addition to the constraint on trainee, session, and device, a retried batch that happ ens to 
include a record the server already stored from an earlier, interrupted attempt is silently 
recognised as a duplicate and does not create a second row, satisfying FR-11 and NFR-5.

3.11 Ethical Considerations 
Because the evaluation plan in Section 3.10 involves deliberately attempting to defraud a 
system using real trainees as participants, and because the comparative evaluation in 
Section 4.1.6 involved real training sessions and real attendance records, this project was 
designed with explicit attention to the ethical treatment of participants throughout. 
Informed consent was obtained individually from every trainee who took part in the fraud 
simulation experiment described in Section 3.10 before any attempt was made. Each 
participant was told, in plain language, that they were being asked to deliberately t ry to 
defeat the attendance system using one of four specified methods, that their participation 
was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any point without any consequence to their 
standing at CivilSalt Bamenda, and that the results of their individual a ttempts would be 
recorded anonymously by attack strategy rather than by name. A copy of the consent 
script used is included as Appendix G. 
No participant was asked to do anything that could plausibly cause them harm, 
embarrassment, or academic or professional disadvantage. The phone -lending strategy, 
described in Section 3.10 and Section 4.1.2, was conducted using the researcher's own 
test devices and volunteer pairs of trainees who agreed in advance to the specific scenario, 
rather than by asking any trainee to use a colleague's real personal phone without that 
colleague's knowledge. No participant's genuine attendance record was placed at ri sk 
during testing; all fraud -simulation attempts were recorded to a clearly separated test 
dataset and were never merged with the authentic attendance records used for the 
comparative evaluation in Section 4.1.6 or with CivilSalt Bamenda's own administrative 
attendance records.

processes personally identifiable information, namely trainee names, registration 
numbers, and device identifiers. All test data collected during the experiments was stored 
on the same access -controlled PostgreSQL instance described in Table 3.5, was not 
shared with any party outside the research team and the field supervisor, and was retained

4.2 Discussion 
4.2.1 Discussion of Fraud Prevention 
The overall fraud detection rate of 98.3 per cent across 120 controlled attempts represents 
a strong result. It is significantly higher than the 95 per cent minimum target set in the 
evaluation plan in Table 3.6, and it is higher than any equivalent figure  reported in the 
existing QR attendance literature, where no paper reviewed in Chapter 2 reported any 
fraud detection rate at all. 
The 100 per cent detection rate against screenshot sharing, screen mirroring, and manual 
payload entry confirms that the combination of HMAC -SHA256 signing and device 
UUID binding is effective against the most common and technically accessible forms of 
proxy cheating. These are the attacks that most trainees would think of and attempt first. 
A trainee who does not have deep technical knowledge cannot forge a valid HMAC 
signature without the session secret, and cannot bypass the device UUID check without 
physically possessing the registered phone. 
The two successful attacks in the physical phone -lending scenario represent the most 
important limitation of the device -binding approach. This attack requires physical co -
operation between two trainees and physical movement of the registered device. Arora et 
al. (2021) noted the same limitation in their device fingerprinting research: any security 
mechanism that ties authentication to a physical token is vulnerable if that token can be 
shared. In practice, however, this attack is much harder to carry out un detected in a real 
training hall than the simple screenshot attacks, because the registered trainee must visibly 
leave their seat, exit the room, and return, all during a live session. 
The timing data in Table 4.2 is what makes it possible to reason precisely about how to 
close this gap. Both successful phone -lending attacks completed inside the first two 
minutes of the fifteen -minute Standard window, while all 28 blocked attempts took a t 
least three minutes. This is direct experimental evidence that reducing the expiry window, 
as implemented for high-stakes sessions in Section 3.5.6, would remove the specific time 
margin that allowed these two attempts to succeed: a five -minute High-Stakes window 
still gives an attacker less than half of the margin that a two -minute successful attempt 
used, and a realistic round trip out of a training hall, down a corridor, and back is unlikely 
to complete inside two minutes if the corridor and colleague are not already positioned in 
--- PAGE 82 ---
 
64 
advance, which is itself a much more conspicuous and organisationally difficult act to 
carry out during an assessed session than during an ordinary daily session. The 
configurable expiry window in Section 3.5.6 therefore directly targets the exact 
vulnerability window that the experimental data identified, rather than reducing the 
window uniformly for every session and inconveniencing trainees in low-stakes contexts 
for no measurable security benefit. 
Reducing the expiry window narrows the phone -lending gap without closing it 
completely, because a sufficiently fast and pre-positioned attacker could in principle still 
succeed inside a five -minute window. As discussed at length in Section 3.6.5, the only 
mechanism identified in this project that would close the gap entirely is a biometric 
confirmation step at the moment of scanning, using Flutter's local_auth package to require 
the phone's own owner, and not merely its physical possessor, to confirm the scan through 
the device's existing fingerprint or face -recognition hardware. The configurable expiry 
window and the future biometric step are therefore best understood as complementary 
rather than competing mitigations: the expiry window is available immedia tely, requires 
no new permissions or hardware calls, and meaningfully reduces the attack window 
today, while the biometric step is the more complete but not -yet-implemented solution 
recommended for future development in Section 5.2. 
4.2.2 Discussion of Synchronisation Results 
The overall synchronisation success rate of 99.5 per cent across 1,300 records is a strong 
result that exceeds the 98 per cent target. The key observation is that the failures were all 
caused by specific, identifiable conditions rather than by general unre liability of the 
synchronisation mechanism. Clock manipulation caused 2 failures in Profile 3, an 
application-level upload timeout caused 1 failure in Profile 2, and a server -side database 
lock caused 1 failure in Profile 6. None of these represent failure s of the core 
synchronisation protocol design. 
The zero duplicate result across all 50 test cycles is particularly significant. The scenario 
in Profile 5, where the network connection was cut during an upload and the phone retried 
with the same records, is exactly the kind of situation that causes dupl icate records in 
systems that do not use idempotency keys. In those systems, the server might have 
partially processed the first upload before the connection dropped, and then the second 
--- PAGE 83 ---
 
65 
upload creates additional copies of whatever the first upload had already written. In this 
system, the idempotency key prevents this entirely. The server finds the existing records 
by their idempotency keys and discards the duplicates without producing errors. 
The finding is consistent with Kleppmann and Beresford (2019), who showed that 
idempotency-key-based deduplication is a simple and reliable approach for event -log 
data. Their theoretical analysis predicted that this approach would handle concurrent 
submissions and retries correctly, and the experimental results in this project confirm that 
it does so in practice. 
The single database-locking failure in Profile 6 deserves particular attention because of 
the modest test server specification reported in Table 3.5. A 2 vCPU, 4 GB RAM 
PostgreSQL instance with default connection -handling settings is a plausible bottleneck  
when three devices attempt near -simultaneous write batches, and reporting this 
specification explicitly, rather than describing the server only as “a cloud server”, allows 
this explanation to be evaluated on its merits rather than taken on faith. At the same time, 
honesty about the evidence available requires stating clearly that no CPU, memory, or 
lock-contention benchmarking was captured at the moment of the failure, so the under -
provisioning explanation, while the most plausible one available, remains a n informed 
inference rather than a confirmed diagnosis. Re -running Profile 6 on a more generously 
provisioned instance, ideally with connection pooling enabled through a tool such as 
PgBouncer, would either confirm this explanation by eliminating the failu re or reveal a 
deeper protocol-level issue that would need further investigation; this specific re -test is 
recommended as an immediate item of future work in Section 5.2 rather than claimed 
here as a result already obtained. 
The 11.4-second average latency in Profile 5, the highest observed, is still well within the 
30-second target and is not a concern for practical use. Even in the worst case tested, a 
trainee who reconnects after hours of offline attendance recording will h ave all their 
records confirmed on the server in under 12 seconds. Future optimisation could reduce 
this further by using HTTP/2 persistent connections or by breaking very large batches 
into smaller parallel requests, but neither change is necessary to meet the current targets.

4.2.4 Threats to Validity 
Beyond the four limitations discussed fully in Section 5.1.5, it is useful to consider the 
results in Chapter 4 in terms of the standard categories of validity used in empirical 
software engineering research, namely construct, internal, and external validi ty, to be 
explicit about exactly what kind of confidence the reported figures support. 
Construct validity concerns whether the metrics defined in Table 3.6 actually measure the 
concepts they are intended to measure. The fraud detection rate is measured directly as a 
count of blocked versus successful attempts, which is a direct and largely u nambiguous 
operationalisation of the underlying concept of fraud resistance. The usability rating, by 
contrast, is a single five-point post-session score, which is a coarser and more subjective 
construct than the others; a more elaborate instrument, such a s the multi -item System 
--- PAGE 85 ---
 
67 
Usability Scale used in some human-computer interaction research, might have captured 
a richer picture of the usability construct than the single rating collected here, and this is 
acknowledged as a legitimate simplification made for practicality within th e scope of a 
single project. 
Internal validity concerns whether the experimental design itself supports the causal 
claims made from it. The fraud simulation and synchronisation experiments were 
conducted under directly controlled conditions, with the researcher supervising every 
attempt and recording outcomes immediately, which supports reasonably strong internal 
validity for those two experiments. The comparative evaluation against manual 
attendance in Section 4.1.6, however, necessarily involved introducing simulated proxy 
signatures into the paper register under the researcher's control, since real proxy 
attendance could not ethically or practically be solicited from trainees against their own 
paper records; this means the manual-attendance figures for proxy detection specifically 
should be read as an estimate of what a paper register would catch under a comparable 
rate of proxy attempts, rather than as a naturalistic measurement of proxy attendance that 
occurred entirely independently of the study. 
External validity concerns how far the results generalise beyond the specific conditions 
tested, and is the category most directly addressed by the limitations already set out in 
Section 5.1.5: a single organisation, a single 40 -trainee cohort, an under -benchmarked 
test server, an Android-only implementation, and a fraud simulation that, while covering 
four attack strategies, cannot claim to have anticipated every conceivable method a 
determined and technically sophisticated attacker might attempt in the fu ture. The 
recommendations in Section 5.2 for a larger, multi -organisation evaluation and for the 
specific Profile 6 re -test are the concrete response to these external validity concerns, 
rather than a claim that the current results are somehow insufficient  or unreliable within 
the scope in which they were obtained.

CHAPTER 5: CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 
5.1 Conclusions 
This project set out to design, implement, and experimentally evaluate an offline-capable 
smart attendance system using QR codes and secure synchronisation for CivilSalt 
Bamenda. All four specific objectives were met, all eight evaluation metrics passed their 
minimum targets, and the experimental results confirm that the system outperforms both 
existing online QR code systems and manual paper registers across every measured 
dimension. 
5.1.1 Conclusion on Objective 1: QR Code Module 
The first objective was to design and implement a QR code module that produces time -
limited, HMAC -SHA256 cryptographically signed, session -specific codes that can be 
fully validated on the trainee's device without any server connectivity. This objective was 
met fully. The QR payload format encodes the session ID, programme code, expiry 
timestamp, and HMAC -SHA256 signature, and the step -by-step generation and 
verification pipeline is described in full in Sections 3.5.2 and 3.5.5. The session secret 
required to produce the signature is held only on the server and, transiently, the 
Facilitator's phone, making it impossible for a trainee to forge a valid code. The five-step 
local validation process runs in an average of 1.4 seconds on the lowest-specification test 
device, well within the two -second target. The expiry mechanism blocked 100 per cent 
of post-expiry scan attempts in testing, and, following the corrections made in response 
to the supervisory review, the expiry window itself is now a configurable per -session 
parameter rather than a fixed constant, as described in Section 3.5.6. 
5.1.2 Conclusion on Objective 2: Offline-First Mobile Application 
The second objective was to develop an offline -first mobile application that captures, 
validates, and stores attendance records locally using SQLite without requiring server 
connectivity. This objective was met fully. The application remained completely 
functional across 100 consecutive offline scan sessions on each of three test devices, with 
zero crashes. Records were stored reliably in the local SQLite database with the correct 
schema and constraints. The pending_sync flag system correctly tracked which records 
--- PAGE 88 ---
 
70 
were still waiting to be uploaded. The app's offline mode was indistinguishable from its 
online mode from the trainee's perspective: the scanner, the confirmation message, and 
the history screen all worked exactly the same way regardless of connectivity. 
5.1.3 Conclusion on Objective 3: Security Mechanisms 
The third objective was to implement and evaluate device -binding and duplication -
prevention security mechanisms, measuring their effectiveness through controlled proxy 
attendance simulation experiments. This objective was met. The fraud detection rate 
across 120 controlled proxy attempts was 98.3 per cent, exceeding the 95 per cent target. 
Screenshot sharing, screen mirroring, and manual payload entry were blocked 100 per 
cent of the time by the combination of HMAC signature verification and device UUID 
checking. The physical phone-lending attack had a detection rate of 93.3 per cent, 28 of 
30 attempts blocked, meaning 2 of 30 attempts succeeded, both within the first two 
minutes of the expiry window as shown in Table 4.2. This remains the primary limitation 
of the device-binding approach, and Sections 3.5.6, 3.6.5, and 4.2.1 set out, respectively, 
the configurable expiry window now implemented to narrow this gap, and the biometric 
confirmation step identified as the only mechanism capable of closing it entir ely. Zero 
duplicate records were produced in the database across all testing. 
5.1.4 Conclusion on Objective 4: Synchronisation Protocol 
The fourth objective was to design, implement, and evaluate a secure background 
synchronisation protocol, measuring its success rate, latency, and data integrity. This 
objective was met. The protocol achieved a synchronisation success rate of 99.5 per cent 
across 1,300 offline records and 50 test cycles, exceeding the 98 per cent target. The 
average synchronisation latency was 6.7 seconds, well below the 30 -second target. Zero 
duplicate records were found on the server across all 50 test cycles, confirming that the 
idempotency key mechanism is effective. The server-side re-validation pipeline correctly 
detected and rejected 2 records with unusual clock deviations, demonstrating that the 
security layer on the server adds meaningful protection beyond what is c hecked on the 
phone.

5.1.5 General Conclusion 
The main research question asked whether this system could outperform existing online 
QR and manual attendance systems in fraud prevention, synchronisation reliability, and 
data integrity in low -connectivity environments. The experimental results answer th is 
question positively. Compared with the paper register, the system reduced attendance 
recording time by 71 per cent, detected all five simulated proxy attempts that the paper 
register missed entirely, and produced zero data errors versus three in the paper sessions. 
Compared with online QR systems as described in the literature, the system adds full 
offline capability and a rigorous multi -metric experimental evaluation that confirms the 
system's effectiveness under real-world conditions. 
The system is ready for deployment at CivilSalt Bamenda and at comparable technology 
training organisations in Cameroon and across sub -Saharan Africa. It requires no 
hardware investment beyond the Android phones that trainees and facilitators already 
own. It can be installed from an APK file without needing the Google Play Store. It works 
under the network conditions that are normal at CivilSalt Bamenda, including extended 
periods of complete offline operation. 
These conclusions must, however, be read together with four specific limitations, clarified 
here in direct response to the supervisory review conducted prior to public defence. First, 
the evaluation was conducted with a single cohort of 40 trainees within a single 
organisation, CivilSalt Bamenda, rather than across multiple organisations or multiple 
cohorts of differing sizes. The fraud simulation and synchronisation experiments involved 
a controlled rather than a fully naturalistic setting, and the compara tive evaluation 
involved only these 40 trainees across five sessions. While the results are internally 
consistent and meet all eight pre -defined targets, generalisation claims should be 
tempered accordingly pending a larger, multi-department or multi-organisation study, and 
the figures reported in Chapter 4 should be read as evidence obtained at one deployment 
site rather than as universally representative performance figures. 
Second, as reported in Table 3.5 and discussed in Section 4.2.2, the synchronisation test 
server was a modest 2 vCPU, 4 GB RAM cloud instance running PostgreSQL 14 with 
default settings, and the single database -locking failure observed in the Profile 6 
concurrent multi -device test is most plausibly attributed to this under -provisioning, 
although no CPU, memory, or lock -contention benchmarking data was collected at the 
--- PAGE 90 ---
 
72 
time to confirm this explanation directly. A production deployment on a more generously 
specified and properly tuned server should not encounter this issue, but, in the absence of 
confirming benchmarking evidence, this remains a reasoned inference rather t han a 
demonstrated fact, and Section 5.2 below carries this forward as a specific 
recommendation for further testing. 
Third, the physical phone-lending attack cannot be fully blocked by software alone. This 
is a limitation shared by all device -binding systems and is discussed fully in Sections 
3.6.5 and 4.2.1. The implication is that the reported overall fraud detection r ate of 98.3 
per cent should be understood as the rate achievable against software-based attacks when 
device binding, HMAC-SHA256 signing, and a time-limited expiry window are in place. 
Any scenario in which two trainees physically collaborate using the registered device falls

biometric verification step, whose feasibility is discussed in Section 3.6.5. 
Fourth, the system has only been tested on Android, and this should be stated plainly

The Flutter codebase supports cross -platform compilation in principle, but iOS imposes 
materially stricter restrictions on background execution than Android does, and no iOS-
specific background synchronisation implementation has been written or tested for this 
project. Readers, examiners, and any organisation considering deployment should 
therefore not read the reported synchronisation results, in particular the 99.5 pe r cent 
success rate and 6.7-second average latency in Section 4.1.3, as applying to iOS devices; 
they describe Android behaviour only, and a separate implementation and a separate 
round of testing using iOS's BackgroundTasks framework would be required bef ore any 
equivalent claim could be made for iOS.

5.2 Recommendations 
Based on the results, the limitations identified during testing, and the corrections 
requested during the supervisory review prior to public defence, the following 
recommendations are made for deployment and future development. 
1. The QR code expiry window should remain configurable by the Facilitator when 
creating a session, as implemented in Section 3.5.6 in direct response to the 
supervisory recommendation. The default should remain 15 minutes for regular 
training sessions. For h igh-stakes sessions such as end -of-cohort assessment 
attendance checks, the Facilitator should continue to use the 5 -minute High-Stakes 
preset. This closes the specific two-minute time margin identified in Table 4.2 more 
effectively without inconveniencing  trainees in normal training sessions, and any 
future deployment should retain this as a per -session choice rather than reverting to 
a single fixed value. 
2. A biometric confirmation step should be added at scan time in a future version of the 
app, as analysed in detail in Section 3.6.5 in direct response to the supervisory 
recommendation. Using Flutter's local_auth package, the app should require a 
fingerprint or face -recognition confirmation immediately after the QR scan is 
confirmed valid but before the record is written to the database. This is the only 
mechanism identified in this project that would raise the fraud detection rate to 
effectively 100 per cent by making it impossible for anyone other than the registered 
trainee's own biometric owner to record attendance, even when that person is holding 
the registered phone. Implementing and experimentally re -evaluating this step, 
specifically re-running the ph one-lending strategy of the fraud simulation with the 
biometric check enabled, is recommended as the immediate next development 
priority. 
3. The server hardware specification used for future synchronisation testing should 
continue to be reported explicitly, as done in Table 3.5 in direct response to the

synchronisation test, should be re-run on a more generously provisioned PostgreSQL 
instance, for example with at least 8 GB of RAM and connection pooling enabled

captured during the test. This would  either confirm that under-provisioning was the

  parts = rawPayload.split('|') 
  IF parts.length != 4 THEN 
    RETURN ERROR('QR code format is not valid - cannot read the code') 
  END IF 
  sessionIdInQR = parts[0] 
  programmeCode = parts[1] 
  expiryUnix = INTEGER(parts[2]) 
  signatureInQR = parts[3] 
  
  // STEP 2: Verify the HMAC-SHA256 signature 
  payloadBase = sessionIdInQR + '|' + programmeCode + '|' + expiryUnix 
  expectedSig = HMAC_SHA256(payloadBase, cachedSecret) 
  IF expectedSig != signatureInQR THEN 
    RETURN ERROR('QR code is not valid - it may have been tampered with or shared') 
  END IF 
  
  // STEP 3: Check whether the QR code has expired 
  currentTime = getCurrentUnixTimestamp() 
  CLOCK_TOLERANCE = 300  // 5 minutes allowed for clock differences 
  IF currentTime > (expiryUnix + CLOCK_TOLERANCE) THEN 
    RETURN ERROR('QR code has expired - ask the facilitator to refresh it') 
  END IF 
  
  // STEP 4: Check whether this is the registered device 
  IF getDeviceUUID() != deviceUUID THEN 
    RETURN ERROR('This phone is not registered to your account') 
  END IF 
  
  // STEP 5: Check for duplicate in the local database 
  IF localDatabase.exists(traineeId, sessionId, deviceUUID) THEN 
    RETURN ERROR('Attendance for this session is already recorded on this device') 
  END IF 
  
  // All checks passed: save the record locally

79 
  idempotencyKey = deviceUUID + '|' + sessionId + '|' + (currentTime * 1000) 
  localDatabase.insert(traineeId, sessionId, deviceUUID, currentTime, idempotencyKey) 
  RETURN SUCCESS('Attendance recorded') 
  
END FUNCTION

The two code excerpts below show the actual implementation of the HMAC -SHA256

H.1 Server-side payload signing (Python / Django) 
import hmac, hashlib, time, secrets 
  
def generate_session_secret(): 
    # 256-bit cryptographically secure random key, per Section 3.5.2 Step 1 
    return secrets.token_hex(32) 
  
def build_signed_payload(session_id, programme_code, session_secret, expiry_window_seconds):  
    expiry_unix = int(time.time()) + expiry_window_seconds 
    payload_base = f"{session_id}|{programme_code}|{expiry_unix}" 
    signature = hmac.new( 
        key=session_secret.encode('utf-8'), 
        msg=payload_base.encode('utf-8'), 
        digestmod=hashlib.sha256 
    ).hexdigest() 
    return f"{payload_base}|{signature}"
